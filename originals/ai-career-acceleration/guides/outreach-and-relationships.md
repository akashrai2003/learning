# Outreach and professional relationships

## Choose a reason to contact someone

Relevant reasons: a role on their team matches your shipped work; their public technical post overlaps a reproduction you completed; an event or repository discussion led to a real conversation; or a recruiter is hiring the exact family you target. A generic shared interest in AI is weak context.

Use direct official applications for jobs and ordinary professional channels for optional context. Keep technical contribution discussions focused on the work. This pack contains editable outreach examples, but sends nothing and identifies no private recipients.

## A concise job-context note

'Hi [name], I saw [role/team]. I work on production AI systems spanning document workflows, Python services and local inference. I recently reproduced [specific public result], with [link]. The role's [specific responsibility] looks close to work I have owned. Is the team considering candidates based in India, and would this experience be relevant?'

Use only true completed work and a verified role. Remove a sentence if it adds no context. Do not attach an entire architecture essay to a first message.

## A contextual referral request

After a real exchange: 'Thanks for the discussion on [topic]. I am applying to [specific role]. My strongest match is [evidence]. If you are comfortable referring me, I can send a concise resume and the role link; no problem if you do not know my work well enough yet.'

A referral should not pressure someone to vouch for unobserved skills. Ask once and respect no response.

## Recruiter introduction

'I am an AI engineer in Bengaluru with approximately two years across [accurate scope]. My strongest experience is [two relevant areas]. I am exploring [role family] positions in India and international teams able to hire remotely from India. Here is a concise project example: [link]. Could you share the location eligibility, level and compensation range for [role]?'

Do not lead every first interaction with compensation, but qualify the range before a long mismatched process. Current salary is not your proof of value.

## Technical conversation

When replying to an engineer's post, add a concrete observation or question: what configuration was measured, how cache effects were controlled, or a small counterexample. Do not paste a polished generic compliment and ask for a referral. If your investigation is unfinished, state that.

## Follow-up policy

Suggested cadence: one brief follow-up after roughly 5–7 business days when there is a relevant update or an outstanding question. This is a courtesy heuristic, not a universal platform rule. If there is no reply, archive or wait for new context. Do not send repeated cross-channel reminders or mass-message a team.

## Calls and written updates

For a short call, prepare a two-minute introduction, two evidence links and three precise questions. Afterward send a short thank-you only if appropriate, with any promised material. Record next action and due date in your tracker. Do not claim someone endorsed you because they replied once.

## Community participation

Attend a relevant public meetup, technical office hour or project community event when available. Ask a question arising from actual use. Volunteer for a small useful task if you have the capacity. Relationships usually develop from repeated useful interactions, but no contribution guarantees employment or referrals.

## Avoiding unnecessary spend

Start with free public channels and direct applications. A paid subscription, course or conference should solve a specific bottleneck, not serve as a substitute for evidence. Measure whether the activity produces learning, useful conversations or qualified processes before allocating more time or money.
