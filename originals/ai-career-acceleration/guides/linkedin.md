# LinkedIn visibility based on engineering evidence

## The audience and the job

Write for engineers, engineering managers, technical recruiters and founders hiring for production AI systems. Your objective is that a relevant person can understand what you did, inspect the evidence and remember your specialty. Broad reach without relevant conversations is a weak career signal.

LinkedIn's official 2026 guidance emphasizes authentic perspective and discourages generic automated content [L1]. Its help page describes post analytics [L2]. Neither source establishes a guaranteed posting time, external-link trick or follower threshold for employment. Treat the posting cadence below as an experiment, not an algorithm claim.

## Profile structure

Headline draft: **AI Engineer | Production LLM systems, document intelligence and inference serving | Python, FastAPI, vLLM**. Add edge deployment once you have an artifact. Do not fill the headline with every library you've encountered or call yourself a kernel specialist prematurely.

About structure: one sentence on what you build; a paragraph on two production problems you owned; two or three verifiable results; a sentence on current public work; and the opportunities you seek. Explicitly state 'Based in Bengaluru; open to India roles and global teams able to hire remotely in India' if that reflects your preferences. Avoid putting current salary on the public profile.

Featured section: one concise technical case study, the flagship repository, a measured benchmark report, and optionally a merged contribution. A tutorial certificate can be secondary. The first screen should answer what you build and where the evidence is.

Experience entries should explain scope and personal responsibility. Label internships and full-time periods accurately. Check that resume, LinkedIn and application dates agree. Two years of overlapping projects do not become four years of employment.

## Your four content pillars

1. **Production inference:** latency, context budgets, memory residency, deployment failures and profiling.
2. **Document AI correctness:** table alignment, source evidence, disagreement evaluation and grounded extraction.
3. **Open-source engineering:** reproductions, regression tests, code review lessons and maintainers' accepted changes.
4. **Learning in public:** a C++ lifetime bug, cache implementation or Android measurement, with a clear learning label.

Start with two posts a week: one evidence-based result and one narrower lesson or investigation. If results are not ready, publish one useful post rather than inventing progress. Use an occasional longer article as the canonical case study and link shorter posts to it.

## The anatomy of a useful technical post

Begin with a concrete engineering problem: 'I measured whether keeping a small model resident changes application TTFT.' Then give the test setup, one result or failure, your interpretation, the main limitation, and a link to methods/code when shareable. A specific question invites a technical exchange: 'How do you distinguish preparation time from first-use cache effects on this backend?'

Avoid unsupported superlatives, universal speedup claims, generic motivational introductions and fabricated incidents. Distinguish reproduction from original discovery. Credit upstream work and coauthors. If an experiment has only three runs, say so instead of presenting a robust tail-latency conclusion.

## Formats

A short text post can explain an incident. A small chart can compare measured configurations. A code screenshot should be accompanied by selectable code or a repository link. A 30–60 second demo can show an offline request and cancellation. A document carousel can explain one concept with an experimental example; use it only if creating it does not consume the implementation time.

Do not make all posts a 'day N of learning' diary. Convert progress into something a practitioner can use: a diagnostic checklist, reproducible test, failure boundary or implementation tradeoff.

## Relationships and reach

Follow engineers working on your chosen repos and companies. Leave a few comments a week only where you can add a relevant observation, precise question or small reproduction. Read the linked work before commenting. Do not use engagement pods, automated comments or broad tagging. Maintainers are collaborators, not an audience you are entitled to recruit.

When someone engages substantively, reply with context. If a professional conversation develops, ask about their technical work or team before requesting favors. You may share a relevant application once, but do not turn every discussion into a referral request.

## Measurement

For each post record topic, artifact, time spent, impressions/reach if available, relevant technical replies, profile visits if available, evidence-link engagement if available, useful conversations and any attributed interview lead. Some platform fields vary; leave unavailable fields blank. Do not attribute every recruiter message to the latest post.

After four weeks compare topics and formats within your own modest sample. Keep what produces relevant conversations and credible evidence. Do not optimize solely for likes or conclude a universal best time from eight posts.

## Publishing boundaries for your experience

Your defense-related work requires particular care with what you can share. Use public/synthetic documents, public checkpoints and independently written code for the public artifact. Changing a client name does not make private data or employer-owned code publishable. Share a general engineering principle only within what you are allowed to disclose. This is a practical boundary for your actual work context, not a reason to avoid publishing safe independent experiments.
